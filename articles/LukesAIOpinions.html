LLM as a Judge -

        The first order of business I’d like to talk about would be my experience with LLM being used as a judge. In this particular instance the goal that I worked toward was using Claud to judge DHCPv6 Conformance compatibility of devices. We provided a modern claude model with the following:

  • A JSON conversion of wireshark capture detailing device behavior
  • Access to the DHCPv6 Conformance Test Specification that it was able to pull test cases from using tool calls
  • Access to the DHCPv6 RFC which is was able to fetch sections of through tool calls as well
  • Tool calls designed for it to be able to handle mathematical calculations that it could not properly perform

The results of this were as I somewhat expected. The AI was roughly around 90% accurate in terms of detecting Passing behavior with it sometimes failing tests by making assumptions about pieces of information instead of using the tool calls provided to it. An example of this behavior was when it tried to find the time between messages. The times were 4.137s and 2.132s. Instead of using the tool call that was set up for it and told to use, it would instead try to perform its own incorrect calculation and result in failing behavior.

I think that this is a great example of some of the current limitations to how LLMs actually work in the present day. Keep in mind that while you may think of an LLM as a crazy powerful thinking computer mind, it is really just trying to figure out the next best thing to say to satisfy the criteria given to it and to make the user happy. When it comes to math, finding the next best answer usually ends up being a response that is an answer to a more commonly asked problem instead of the real answer because the more common answer would have better “user satisfaction”.

My final critique of LLM as a judge comes from many AI workflows I have seen people bragging and posting about online. While I do believe that AI workflows can be a very useful tool, I find that a lot of these workflows take resources to put into place and use than they are actually worth. A common model of a workflow I've seen is having an LLM such as Claude verify the integrity of its own responses. This is a common misconception that LLMs have this magical ability to find mistakes present in their code that it didn't notice the first time. As a quick hint to my experience with this: if an LLM doesn't realize a mistake the first time around, it won't notice ever. Despite this fact it does not stop attempts from users online to send upwards of 10 api calls instead of just 1 to get the same result.

Data Centers and Costs -

        Within the coming years AI is really going to hit a wall, a giant wall that we call a paywall. Most AI models that are currently used are mostly free or charging based on token usage. This model is likely what will persist into the future, however the price these AI companies are charging for tokens is completely unsustainable for the future. Many companies now undercharge users compared to the actual amount of money that the company is spending to give them their product. This means that a price increase in the future is inevitable and could cause a potential shift away from the AI centered market sway we've seen in recent years. Overall I think that the much better solution that many companies will begin to implement is a mixture of local AI models and large scale LLMs.

        One of the key points I learned in my experience working with AI is that the tool you are using should scale for the job. “Dont use an excavator for a potted plant” is a common analogy we used while working with these models. For example, if you wanted a model to simply search through a list of pdfs, a locally hosted QWEN model would do just fine. If you need something with more complex thinking or problem solving a claud model would be much better for the task. I think this is an optimization that will become just as crucial to companies as implementing AI in the first place.

        With the culmination of these topics it's finally time to talk about Data Centers. For those who have the pleasure of not knowing, let me fill you in on what a data center is and what its job is with ai. Think of AI like an app. It's a very expensive and hard to run app. AI uses lots of resources and can't really be run on your normal laptop or desktop. This is where data centers come in. A data center is a collection of very high speed computers designed specifically to run AI models. Instead of buying one of these high speed computers, people instead opt to pay a company with a data center a small amount of money to use theirs. The issue with these Data Centers often comes from their community and environmental impacts they can have on where they are built. I’m not an environmental expert, so I won't delve further into that topic.

        The biggest issue I see with these data centers is the potential fall off of large scale AI tools when prices inevitably increase. When people learn to optimize AI tools and workflows to run locally as much as possible, the necessity for these large structures becomes less and less and their profits go down. My speculation is that as AI progresses we will see less cloud usage through data centers and more companies opting in to using locally hosted models to avoid “using an excavator for a potted plant”
